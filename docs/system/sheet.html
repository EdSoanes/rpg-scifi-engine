System 08 · Core mechanics

Sheet

How the character sheet, on a tablet or monitor, shows turns, pools, pace and cost so a player can grasp and manage them without knowing the rules. Concept, not prototype.

What this document is

  • A visual concept for the part of the character sheet that handles time in a fight: the turn, the two pools, the cost of an action, commitments across turns, going back.
  • It identifies what the player must always see, what they must be able to do, and one way to lay it out. It is not a screen design and nothing here is pixel-accurate.
  • Target: a tablet in landscape, or a monitor. Phones are not supported; the game has too much to show. Everything below assumes about 1000 points of width and enough height for the ledger and the work surface at once. The GM still calls turns out loud; the sheet never waits on another device.
  • The rest of the sheet (skills, gear, injuries, the matrix view) is out of scope here. It hangs off the same spine.
Design test

A new player should be able to answer four questions by looking, without reading: whose turn is it, what can I still do, what will this cost me, and how do I take that back.

One metaphor: pips and a ledger

  • Pips are the unit of everything in a turn. Tempo and Focus are rows of pips. An action's cost is a number of pips. Pace changes how many pips. Overdrive is pips you don't have. A multi-turn action is pips paid over several turns.
  • The ledger is the turn timeline: one column per turn, what was spent and done in each, what is still running. Past columns are the undo control. Future columns show what is already committed and what will expire.
  • Nothing is shown as a probability number. Odds are words, costs are pips, time is turns or intervals.
  • Body and mind have a colour each, used for pips, action blocks and chips. Nothing else in the sheet uses those two colours.
Why pips and not numbers

"You have 4 Tempo, this costs 2" is arithmetic. Four dots with two of them about to dim is a glance. Pips also make reservations visible: a pip ringed for a dodge is a decision the player can see they made. Physical tokens on a paper sheet do the same job, and players already know how to read them.

  • Selecting anything, in any region, puts it in the detail panel: an action, a pace row, a block in the ledger, a pip, a running bar. The panel never needs to be opened.
  • The ledger shows as many turns as fit, with the current column kept in view. On a monitor that is a whole fight; on a tablet, eight to ten turns, scrolling horizontally for more.
  • The regions do not move or resize during play. A player learns where things are once.
  • The rest of the character (skills, gear, injuries, the matrix view) is reached by tabs on the work surface. The turn strip, the ledger and the left rail stay in place whichever tab is open, so a player browsing their gear still sees the turn.
Why not a phone

The four things a player must see at once (whose turn, what is left, what this costs, how to take it back) need the ledger and the work surface on screen together, and the detail panel is what makes the transparency feature usable rather than theoretical. On a phone every one of those became a drawer or a modal. A tablet shows them all and still has room for the rest of the character.

  • A refund from a strong success lights a pip back up, with a short label. The ledger records it as "time saved".
  • A bonus pool from a stim or an implant extends the row with pips of the same colour and a label under them, for as long as the effect lasts.
  • A pip changed by hand (the GM gives one back) is marked with a small dot, as every hand change on the sheet is.
Why spent pips stay visible

If the row shrank, the player would lose the sense of how big their turn was and where it went. A dimmed pip is a receipt. It also gives undo a target: tap the pip, see the action, take it back.

The pace picker

  • One row per pace. Every row shows the same four things: which pips go, the odds as a word, whether a bad failure is possible, and when the action resolves.
  • The pips in the cost column are the player's actual remaining pips, with the ones this pace would take drawn as ghosts. Kept pips are shown ringed and not ghosted. The same ghosts appear on the pool row behind the card.
  • A pace that needs more pips than the pool has shows the extra beyond a divider labelled with when they are paid: "next turn" for a multi-turn action, or dashed warning rings for overdrive if the player chooses that instead.
  • A pace that is not offered stays in the list, greyed, with its reason in words. The player can still tap it; the sheet asks once and then does it. Nothing is refused.
  • Certain and impossible paces say so in place of an odds word and show no roll.
  • The pace rows expand in place under the action on the work surface; the figure above shows them laid out as a card for legibility. Confirming is one tap on the selected row's button. The ghosts become spent pips in the left rail, a block appears in the current column of the ledger, and the detail panel shows the pending roll.
Why the four columns and nothing else

Cost, chance, safety and time are the whole decision. The dice expression, the rating breakdown and the opponent's numbers are shown too, but in the detail panel beside the rows, not inside them. The rows stay comparable at a glance; the derivation is there for whoever looks right.

  • The ledger is always open. The current column stays in view; older columns scroll off to the left and can be scrolled back to.
  • Advancing a turn adds a column and refills the pools. The previous column stops being editable except through rewind.
  • The GM's "advance to turn N" and "renumber" are reached from the turn strip, not the ledger. Renumbering shifts every column label and nothing else.
  • Selecting a block or a bar describes it in the detail panel, where its undo, redo-roll and switch-off controls live.
Why the timeline is also the undo

The engine goes back to the start of a turn, never to a point inside one. A column is exactly that unit. Making the columns the control means a player who wants to take back a turn looks at the thing that shows the turn, not for a menu. It also makes the consequence visible: everything to the right of the column they tap will go.

Going back

  • Tapping a past column ghosts everything to its right and asks one question in the detail panel. It lists what will be undone in the player's words: actions, rolls, hand changes. It also lists what stays, so a player is not surprised that bleeding is still on.
  • Going back to the current turn is the same gesture on the current column: "undo this turn so far".
  • After going back, the column becomes the current one, pools refill to what they were at its start, and the player replays. The discarded columns are gone; the engine keeps no branch.
  • A single action can be undone without going back, from its block, when nothing after it depends on it. The sheet says when it does not qualify and offers the rewind instead.
  • Pending rolls that belonged to discarded turns disappear with them. The badge updates.
Why ask at all when the engine never refuses

Going back is not refused; it is irreversible, and that is the one case where a question earns its tap. Everything else on the sheet acts at once and can be taken back.

Reacting out of turn

  • The player opens this themselves when the GM says they are attacked. There is no network; nothing arrives on the sheet.
  • Options are the reactions the character has, each with its pips ghosted and the attacker's odds as a word, against the static value and against a reaction. The attacker's rating is typed or taken from the last time it was typed.
  • Choosing a reaction spends the pips, places an R block in the current column, and queues a roll. The total is read aloud; the attacker types it as their difficulty.
  • With no pips left, the dodge row shows overdrive rings and the fatigue it costs. It is still offered.

Outside turn tracking

  • The turn strip reads "Not counting turns" with the last time event named: "Not counting turns · 6 hours since you started". Its button starts turn tracking at turn one.
  • The pools dim entirely. They are still shown so the player knows where they will be.
  • The timeline strip counts intervals instead of turns. Each extended task is a row: progress, tolerance, the per-interval results, and the pending roll for the current interval. The pace was chosen when the task began and can be changed between rolls.
  • Going back is manual here, as the vision says: the player changes progress by hand, and the change is marked.
  • Something that starts turn tracking mid-task (a guard arrives) switches the strip to turns. The task keeps its progress and continues per turn in the running lane.

Teaching the player

  • The sheet explains in words, in the detail panel, for whatever is selected. Nothing is explained unprompted. Full describe (the tree of modifications) is one tap from the panel.
  • The first fight shows three hints, once each: "these are your pips for this turn", "tap a past turn to take it back", "a ringed pip is kept for a dodge".
  • No rule names in the interface. Not "Tempo pool", not "margin", not "turn tracking". Pips, turns, "take it back", "counting turns".
Open: what the GM app shows

Many sheets, advanced together, each with a timeline. Whether the GM sees one combined ledger or a stack of sheets is not decided and is not needed for the player sheet.

Open: the words for the pools

The rules call them Tempo and Focus. The sheet may only ever say "pips" in two colours and never name them. Decide with the first playable sheet.
